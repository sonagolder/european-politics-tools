Forming a government in the Weimar Reichstag

Your job is the one every German chancellor faced between 1928 and 1933: assemble a cabinet that can command more than half the seats.     What actually happened
    How to read the three tests

    Majority is pure arithmetic. A cabinet that cannot win a vote in the Reichstag can be removed by one, so more than half the seats is the minimum condition for governing normally.

    Pro-republic only is a classification, not a claim about who was willing to govern. The Communists (KPD) and the National Socialists (NSDAP, the Nazi party) both rejected the constitutional order and wanted to replace it. That is why a majority resting on either of them would not have preserved the republic, even though it would have been a majority.

    It would be wrong to say these parties refused to enter government. The Nazis wanted power badly and negotiated for it repeatedly; what Hitler refused was any role below the chancellorship. In August 1932 he was offered the deputy post under the sitting chancellor Franz von Papen and turned it down. In November, President Paul von Hindenburg offered him the chancellorship on condition that he assemble a parliamentary majority, and he refused that too, holding out for the power to rule by decree. The exclusion ran in both directions and it was about terms.

    The Communists are a different case again. Following the line set by the Soviet-led international organization of communist parties, which treated Social Democrats as the main enemy, the KPD rejected an SPD proposal to work together in November 1931 and called instead for intensifying the fight against social democracy. Here the estrangement was mutual and ideological rather than a bargaining failure over posts.

    So the tool does not stop you selecting them. It tells you what kind of majority you have built, and leaves the question of why it did not happen to the historical note for that election — which is where the interesting answer lives.

    Connected asks whether your parties sit next to each other on the left–right ordering. Coalition theorists expect governments to be connected, because a party that is ideologically closer to the cabinet than one of its actual members is a cheaper partner and a likelier one. A coalition with a gap in the middle — skipping over a party to reach one further away — is a signal of strain. You will meet this idea again when the course turns to government formation.

    Where these numbers come from

    Seat totals follow the published results for each Reichstag election. The four chambers were different sizes because Weimar's electoral law awarded one seat per 60,000 votes rather than fixing the number of seats, so the chamber grew and shrank with turnout.

    • Grouping. Eight parties are named individually. Everything else is pooled into other small parties, which was in reality a scatter of separate special-interest and regional parties — the business and middle-class Wirtschaftspartei, agrarian lists, regional parties, and several Protestant and conservative splinters. Pooling them makes coalition-building easier than it was, since it treats them as a single bloc that would join or refuse together. When a majority is still out of reach, that result holds all the more strongly for the real chamber.
    • The DNVP. The nationalist DNVP is counted here as an available partner, since it had served in Weimar cabinets in the 1920s. Under Alfred Hugenberg after 1928 it grew openly hostile to the republic. Counting it as available is therefore the generous assumption.
    • The liberal center. The German Democratic Party (DDP) reorganized itself as the German State Party (DStP) in 1930, so the same slot changes name between the first and second elections here.
    • Small discrepancies. Published sources differ by a seat or two on some of the minor parties. Totals here are reconciled so that the parties always sum exactly to the official size of each chamber.
