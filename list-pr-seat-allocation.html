From votes to seats

A party list system promises each party seats in proportion to its votes, but seats come in whole numbers and votes do not. The week 9 lecture's method for closing that gap is the Hare quota with largest remainders. Step through it one row at a time on a district you can change, then fill in the table yourself for districts you have not seen.

            How it is checked

            Step 1. Fill in the quota and each party's automatic seats, then check them. If something is wrong, the cells are marked and the tool says what kind of mistake it looks like. Fix them and check again as often as you like, or ask to be shown the answer and go on.

            Step 2. Once the automatic seats are right, the votes ÷ quota and remainder rows are filled in for you. Press a party's button to give it a remainder seat, then check. This check is once only; afterwards the ranking of remainders and the correct totals appear.

            The total-seats row adds up your entries as you go, so you can see whether you have handed out more or fewer seats than the district has. The method itself is on the other tab, worked through step by step.

            Three things that catch people out

            Every district shows at least one of these. Each is checked off once you get both steps right at the first check, without being shown anything, in a district that has it. Districts lean toward whichever you have not got yet.

              All three covered. You have now handled every trap this method sets. The districts do not run out, so keep going for as long as it is useful, but from here they are practice on cases you have already met.

              Why the remainder seats are not just rounding

              Rounding each party's votes ÷ quota to the nearest whole number looks like a shortcut to the same answer. It is not, because nothing makes the rounded numbers add up to the number of seats. In the lecture's ten-seat example, rounding gives seats in a district that has ten. Rounding can also hand out too few, when several parties each sit just under one half.

              The largest remainder method avoids this by fixing the number of remainder seats first — whatever is left after the automatic seats — and then giving them out in order. So the question is never whether a remainder is “big enough” in itself. It is whether it is among the largest, when there are only so many seats to go round.

              And every party is in that ranking, including parties that won no automatic seats at all. For them the remainder is simply their whole votes ÷ quota.

              Two ways of writing a remainder

              The lecture writes a remainder as a fraction of a quota, and so does this tool throughout. The same remainder can be written as a number of votes: multiply the fraction by the quota. With a quota of 10,000, a remainder of 0.7 is 7,000 votes left over after paying for the automatic seats. Within a district the two always rank the parties in the same order, because every party is divided by the same quota.

              Where votes ÷ quota does not come out to a short decimal, the tool cuts it off rather than rounding it and adds an ellipsis — so 1.99… is never shown as 2.00, which would look like a full second quota. The ranking itself always uses the exact values.

              Ties, and other ways of turning votes into seats

              Two parties can have exactly the same remainder at the point where the seats run out. The method as taught in the lecture does not say how to break that tie, so this tool does not invent a rule: it marks the seat as undecided and says which parties are tied. You can make a tie on the first tab by giving two parties the same number of votes.

              The Hare quota with largest remainders is one formula among several. The lecture also gives the Droop quota, which divides the votes by one more than the number of seats, making the quota slightly smaller.               Where these numbers come from

              The method and the ten-seat example are from the week 9 lecture slides on electoral systems. The other ready-made districts and every practice district were made up for this tool. They are not the results of any real election, and none of them is a homework or exam question.

              Practice districts are generated, not chosen. Each one has a whole-number quota and vote totals that divide into it to exactly two decimal places, so the arithmetic is the same kind you will meet in the course rather than long division.
