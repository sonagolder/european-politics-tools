Forming a government

Voters in a parliamentary democracy elect a parliament, not a government. When no party wins a majority of the seats, the parties bargain over who governs. This tool works through the week 12 lecture’s example of how to predict the result, and then gives you elections in invented countries to work through, including some where the government that formed is not one either prediction expects.

How the two predictions are defined here

The office-seeking prediction is the minimum winning coalition: of all the minimal winning coalitions, the one with the fewest seats, and so the fewest surplus seats. Some people call it the least minimal winning coalition. There is only ever one in this tool’s examples.

The policy-seeking prediction is a minimal winning connected coalition: a minimal winning coalition whose parties sit next to each other on the left–right dimension, with no party outside the coalition sitting between two of its members. This follows the lecture, and there can be more than one, so a government matches the policy-seeking prediction if it is any of them.

Both predictions look at every possible government, not only the ones that include the formateur’s party. That is how the lecture and the homework classify coalitions. It also means a prediction can leave out the party that is doing the negotiating, which is worth noticing when it happens.

The tool uses one left–right dimension and only the order of the parties along it, not the distances between them. With two dimensions, as for the German Free Democrats (FDP) in the chapter, it is less clear which coalitions count as connected.

Where these numbers come from

The four-party example in the walkthrough is the one in the week 12 lecture slides. The shares of each type of government are from Figure 12.1 of chapter 12, which covers European governments that formed after elections between 1945 and 2022. The real cases mentioned in the walkthrough are the chapter’s and the lecture’s.

The countries in practice mode are invented. Their parties, seat totals and governments are not the results of any real election, and none of them is a homework question.
