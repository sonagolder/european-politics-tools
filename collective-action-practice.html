Would you take part?

Some good things cannot be kept from anyone once they exist — a government that listens, clean air, a road, a lighthouse, and so on. They arrive only if enough people do something to bring them about, and whoever does that thing pays for it while everyone else gets it anyway. That is the collective action problem. The cost of participating in pro-democracy protests increases in repressive regimes. In the examples below, though, we assume that the benefit always outweighs the cost.

Your progress

The five situations you can be in

Problems land more often on the three in the middle, because those are where the answer changes and where the mistakes are. Until you have all five, they also lean toward whichever are still missing, so you should not have to wait long for the last one. Each is checked off once you have answered it correctly.

    All five covered.

    How to read the table, worked through once

    The table has two rows because you have two choices, and three columns because only three things can be true about everybody else: too few of them for the protest to succeed even with you, exactly enough that you make the difference, or already enough without you.

    Take a group of N = 40 where K = 12 are needed, success is worth B = 10 to each person, turning up costs C = 2, and nobody is punished for staying home. Suppose 11 others are already out there.

    others = 11 K − 1 = 11, so you are in the middle column take part → 11 + 1 = 12 = K → it succeeds you get B and you paid C B − C = 10 − 2 = 8 stay home → 11 < 12 = K → it fails nobody gets anything 0 8 > 0, so you take part.

    Now move one person. If 12 others are already out, the protest succeeds without you: staying home pays B = 10 and taking part pays B − C = 8, so you stay home and let them do it. If only 10 others are out, it fails either way: staying home pays 0 and taking part pays −C = −2, so you stay home again. One person in either direction flips the answer.

    Notice what does the work in the middle column. It is not that you care more, or that the cause is more urgent. It is that the count came out so that you personally are the difference between success and failure.

    What you are assumed to know

    Worth separating what this model gives you from what it leaves open, because the two claims about group size sit exactly on the seam between them.

    Everybody knows how the situation is built — how many people are in the group, how many are needed, what success is worth, what taking part costs. This is called complete information. It holds everywhere in this tool, including under the penalty extension, and nothing here relaxes it.

    Nobody is assumed to know what everyone else will do. This is the part that is easy to get backwards. The table does not tell you what to do; it tells you what to do given an expectation about how many others will take part. Everyone in the group is deciding at the same time, so nobody gets to look first and then choose. Each of the three scenarios is therefore something you expect or conjecture, not something you have watched happen.

    So the table is a complete answer to a conditional question, and it is silent about where your expectation comes from or whether it is any good.

    The problems hand you the number, which is stronger than the model requires. "11 of the other 39 are already taking part" converts a conjecture into a fact, and that is what makes the question answerable and gradeable. The practice tab does the same. It is worth noticing that you are being handed something a person at the edge of a square does not have.

    And this is where the group-size claims live. They are not further facts about the table. They are about the thing the table leaves open — what you can reasonably expect. In a group of 40 you might form some view about whether the count will land on you. In a group of millions you have almost no basis for one, and hardly anybody will conclude they are the critical person. The strip beneath the table is what that looks like counted out: every situation you could be facing, only one of which makes your turning up matter.

    To go further and ask how likely each situation is, you would need a model of where expectations come from and how they shift — genuinely different machinery, and what the course reaches for when it asks why 1989 happened when it did rather than a decade earlier.

    A third question about information. The claim above is about what you expect of everybody else. Monitoring and punishment, which the penalty slider represents, is its mirror image: what everybody else can find out about you. A group small enough to see who stayed away can act on it; a country of millions cannot. None of the three is about how much anyone wants the good.

    Why the group matters: two claims, and a third thing behind them

    The table tells you what to do once you know how many others are taking part. But that is exactly the thing a person at the edge of a square does not know. The lecture makes two claims about how the shape of the group changes the problem, and the strip under the table is where you can watch both of them.

    One. The wider the gap between K and N, the stronger the pull to free-ride. There are N situations you could be in, because the number of others taking part runs from 0 to N − 1. Of those, N − K are situations in which enough others are already out that you can stay home and collect the benefit anyway. So the gap between K and N is the count of situations in which free-riding is available. If a protest needs 12 people out of 40, there are 28 of them; if it needs 38 of 40, there are 2. When only a few contributors are needed out of many possible ones, most of the time you will not be one of the few — so why be one of the ones who pays?

    This has a consequence that may seem counterintuitive. If the pull to free-ride grows with the gap between K and N, then anyone trying to organize collective action should want their members to believe that gap is small — that success depends on nearly everybody rather than on a few. Which is exactly what organizers say. Parties tell supporters that every vote is crucial; public radio pledge drives tell listeners the station depends on their contribution. As arithmetic both are usually false. As things to say to somebody deciding whether to bother, both are useful, because what an organizer needs is participation rather than accuracy.

    Two. The larger N is, the harder it is to know whether you are the critical actor. Notice first that this claim is not about the table. The table tells you your best response to an expectation; this claim is about how easy it is to arrive at one, which the table leaves open entirely. See "What you are assumed to know" above — the distinction matters, because a student who does not make it will think the two claims contradict each other.

    Here is the count behind it. Exactly one of the N situations makes you critical, whatever K is: raising K slides the critical situation to the right and never produces a second one. So the larger the group, the thinner that sliver is as a share of everything that could be happening around you — 1 in 12 is a different proposition from 1 in 100 — and the harder it is to work out from inside a crowd whether you are standing in it.

    One warning. It is a count of situations, not a probability: working out how likely each situation is would need beliefs about what everyone else is doing, which this model does not supply.

    What small groups can do that large ones cannot. That second reason is about enforcement rather than counting. In a small group it is easy to see who contributed and who did not, and therefore possible to do something about it; in a large one it is not. The penalty slider is where this enters the model. Set it above the cost of taking part and watch the table: taking part becomes the better option in every column, including the ones where the protest succeeds without you, and the collective action problem disappears. Set it below the cost and nothing changes at all — a penalty only bites once it is worse than turning up.

    That is a sharp result and worth being suspicious of. It says the problem is not really about how much people want the good, nor about group size on its own — it is about whether the group has any way to make not contributing worse than contributing. Group size then matters twice over: once because it sets how likely you are to be the critical one, and again because it sets whether anyone is in a position to notice you were not there.

    The penalty is an extension. The lecture puts the three-column table on the board without it, and the homework and midterm problems are about that version, which is why the practice tab never uses it. In the wider literature these are called selective incentives: rewards or penalties that reach people according to whether they contributed, and which therefore are not public goods themselves.

    What this has to do with eastern Europe

    For most of the Cold War, people living under Communist governments in eastern Europe had reasons to want a different one and no legal way to ask for one. Yet across four decades the serious challenges to those governments were few, and most of them did not begin in the streets. They began inside the ruling parties, among Communists who wanted a reformed version of the system; crowds came out around those openings rather than creating them. Where crowds eventually came out in numbers — East Berlin in 1953, Budapest in 1956 — the answer was Soviet troops.

    The model in this tool is one account of why. Not because people were content, and not because they misjudged how bad the government was — the model assumes they are right about B, and B is large. The problem is C, and the arithmetic in the third column. Taking part is dangerous, the benefit arrives whether or not you took part, and the one situation in which your own presence decides the outcome is a single case out of N that you cannot identify from inside the crowd. Everybody reasoning this way stays home, and the government's stability looks like consent.

    It is worth putting rough numbers on that. Take East Germany, where N is effectively the whole population — millions of people. K, the number of protesters it eventually took to make the government give way, ran to the hundreds of thousands: large in absolute terms, and a small fraction of the population. That is the least favorable shape the model describes, an enormous N against a K a fraction of its size, so that almost nobody had grounds to think of themselves as the critical person. The cost side was not abstract either. The violent suppression of the East Berlin rising of 1953 stood for decades as a reminder of what taking part could cost. On this account the absence of open opposition was not evidence that the government was popular; it is what the free-rider problem looks like from outside.

    This also gives you something to look for in the exception. The challenge that did begin from below started in a shipyard in Gdańsk in August 1980, where the workers stopped work and occupied the yard, and it produced the independent trade union Solidarity rather than starting from one. Notice the setting: a workforce far smaller than a country, in one enclosed place, where each person could see who had stopped and who had not — which is both of the things the model says are missing elsewhere, since a group that can see who stayed away is also a group that can do something about it. That connection is an interpretation rather than something the lecture or the reading claims, and it is a good thing to argue with.

    Communist rule across the region came to an end in 1989 — in some countries through negotiations between governments and opposition, in others through demonstrations of a size nobody had managed to assemble in the four decades before. Whatever explains the mass demonstrations, it is not this model. The collective action model which suggests that large protests are rare can't also account for a year in which they were not. That is the question the course turns to next.

    Where this model comes from, and what it leaves out

    The model is the one presented in the week's lecture, in the same notation: N people, K needed, a benefit B that goes to everyone if the protest succeeds, a cost C paid only by those who take part, and the assumption that B is greater than C. None of the numbers in this tool are measurements of anything. They are illustrations, and you should be able to change them and watch the argument still work, which is what the sliders are for.

    • The penalty is an addition. The lecture's table has 0, 0 and B along the bottom row, which is what this tool shows when the penalty sits at 0 — its starting value. Everything the penalty does is an extension, added so that the lecture's claim about monitoring and punishment in small groups has somewhere to live in the model rather than only in a sentence. If you are revising for an exam, revise the version with the penalty at 0.
    • Where the penalty comes from is not modeled. Somebody has to notice you were absent and be willing to act on it, and acting costs them something too — which is a second collective action problem sitting underneath the first. This tool treats the penalty as simply given. Whether a group can generate one is most of the interesting question and not one this model can answer.
    • The assumption that B is greater than C. The tool enforces it: the cost slider cannot be pushed to or past the benefit. If C were larger than B, nobody would take part in any situation and there would be nothing to explain. It fails at both ends. For many people under the governments in this week's cases, the honest comparison was between a distant political benefit and a near-certain personal catastrophe. And in a great many ordinary collective action problems C is tiny — the price of a stamp, twenty minutes — which does not rescue anything, because a tiny cost still loses to no cost at all in the third column.
    • The stakes are inert in the table, and that is not the same as irrelevant. Nothing in the table's answer depends on B or C, and the tool says so where the sliders are. It would still be reasonable to think that people are less willing to take part as the cost rises, and more willing as the benefit grows, holding everything else fixed — but that is an intuition sitting alongside the model rather than something the table implies, and telling those two apart is a large part of what working with a model involves. If you want costs and benefits to change the answer, you have to build a model in which they do; the penalty slider is a small example of what that takes.
    • What is known, and what is only expected. N, K, B and C are known to everybody throughout. What nobody is assumed to know is how many others will take part: the table gives your best response to an expectation about that, and says nothing about where the expectation comes from. The claims about group size are about precisely that gap, and the practice problems paper over it by handing you a number. Each of these is labeled where it applies, and there is a panel above on the whole question.
    • K is treated as known and sharp. Real protests do not have a number at which they succeed. Support ebbs, governments split over whether to shoot, and the same crowd that changes nothing on Tuesday brings the government down on Thursday. A single K is a large simplification, and it is the one that makes the middle column exist at all.
    • Everybody is identical. One B and one C for the whole group, and now one P as well. In reality the cost of turning up is far higher for some people than others, and so is the benefit. Some people would go whatever the count was.
    • Everybody decides once, alone, and at the same time. There is no way in this model for a person to watch the square fill up and then decide, and no way for anyone to promise anything to anyone else. Both of those change the answer, and both are things real protests are full of.
    • Nothing here explains what happens when the model breaks. Collective action theory is good at explaining why large protests are rare. It is much less good at explaining the moments when they suddenly are not, which is a question this course comes back to.
    Collective action in political science

    A public good is a good with two properties. It is non-excludable: once it exists, nobody can be kept from enjoying it. And it is non-rivalrous: one person enjoying it does not leave less for anyone else. A more democratic government is a public good in this sense. So is national defense, or clean air, and so are most of the things protests are about.

    Those two properties are what create the problem. If the good arrives whether or not you helped, and your helping does not usually change whether it arrives, then the individually sensible thing is to let other people bear the cost. This is called free-riding, and a collective action problem is any situation where each person reasoning sensibly for themselves produces an outcome worse for everyone than if they had all acted together.

    It is worth being clear about what this does not depend on, because the week's cases can leave a misleading impression. Nothing above mentions danger, police, or a government that punishes anyone. The problem comes from the structure of the good, not from the size of the cost: as long as taking part costs you something and the good arrives without you, staying home wins. That is why the same logic turns up in places where nobody is threatening anybody — why unions struggle to sign up members who already benefit from the contract, why public radio has to hold fundraising drives, why turnout in free elections is a puzzle rather than an obvious duty, and why countries find climate agreements hard to keep. Repression is one setting of C. It makes protest a severe case of a general problem; it is not what makes the problem a problem.

    The argument in its modern form comes from the economist Mancur Olson, whose 1965 book The Logic of Collective Action made the case that a shared interest does not by itself produce collective action, and that large groups are worse at it than small ones — not because their members care less, but because in a large group no single contribution is noticed and nobody can be held to account for withholding one. His account of how groups manage it anyway is what the penalty slider stands for: selective incentives, meaning benefits or punishments that reach people according to whether they contributed. A union hiring hall, a professional body's license, a congregation's good standing, a village's willingness to speak to you — each of these can be withheld from one person in a way the public good itself cannot.

    The consequences run well past protest. It is a standard explanation in the literature for why small, concentrated interests routinely beat large, diffuse ones in democratic politics: a few hundred producers who each gain a great deal from a subsidy will organize, and the millions of consumers who each lose a little will not. You will meet the same logic again in this course when it turns to interest groups, to voting, and to bargaining between European Union member states.
