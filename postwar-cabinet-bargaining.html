Choosing your ministries

You lead a Communist party in an eastern European country in the first years after the Second World War. You are joining a broad coalition cabinet with parties you do not control, and the posts are being divided now. You cannot have everything you want. Choose what to ask for, then find out where your choices leave you three years later.

          How this tool scores your cabinet — open only if you want the answer

          The tool converts your cabinet into four measures of leverage over the state, and then asks whether that combination was enough to convert a share of a coalition government into control of the country.

          Coercion — police, security services, and the local officials who decide whether a rally is permitted or a rival's organizer is detained. Legal instruments — prosecutors and courts, which turn detentions into convictions and convictions into banned parties. Patronage — land, jobs, ration cards, licenses and contracts, which buy loyalty at scale in a country that is hungry. Information — newsprint, radio, censorship and press licensing, which decide whose account of events people hear.

          The rule the tool applies: coercion is necessary, and on its own it is not sufficient. You need it above the threshold, plus at least two of the other three. A cabinet that clears all four is the cleanest version of the strategy, and the tool marks it as such.

          The thresholds and the point values are a stylization of an argument historians make, not a measurement of anything. They encode a claim — that ministries differ in how much state capacity and coercive leverage they hand over, and that this mattered more than which ministries set policy or carried prestige. You should be able to see the claim in the numbers and argue with it. The section below says where the numbers are soft.

          Where these numbers come from

          What each ministry is described as administering is accurate for the period, and the cabinets in the historical notes are real. The list itself is not: it is a composite, standardized across both scenarios, rather than a transcription of either cabinet. The costs, the point values and the thresholds are invented — a way of making an argument playable, not data.

          • Bargaining capital. The budget stands for everything that gave a party weight in the room: its vote share, the presence or absence of Soviet occupation forces, its record in the wartime resistance, and the willingness of its patrons to apply pressure. Compressing all of that into one number is a large simplification. It makes the negotiation look more like a market and less like the coercion it often was, which if anything makes your task in this tool harder than it was in Bucharest or Budapest.
          • Prices. A post costs roughly what other parties would fight to keep, which is why foreign affairs and finance are expensive and posts and telegraphs is cheap. The interior ministry is priced as contested but obtainable. In Hungary it was obtainable only because the Soviet-chaired Allied Control Commission insisted on it; a model in which you can simply buy it understates how much that outside pressure was doing.
          • Deputy ministers. Placing a deputy is worth 40 percent of the ministry, capped at three placements. That figure is a guess standing in for a real and documented tactic: putting reliable people one level below a minister from another party. Its real value varied enormously with the person and the ministry.
          • Defense. The armed forces score low on coercion here. In these years the armies were small, supervised by Allied control machinery, and officered largely by men from the old regimes, which is why the political police mattered more than the army. Where a Communist party did later get the army, it usually got it after it already had the police.
          • The premiership. Where it can be taken at all — Czechoslovakia — it scores zero on every measure, and in Hungary it is not on offer, so you never get to test it. That zero is the single most convenient assumption in the tool: it is the assumption that most strengthens the argument the tool is making, and you should treat it with some skepticism. Setting the cabinet's agenda, and controlling who fills a ministry that falls vacant, are ways of shaping what the state does, and in Prague in February 1948 they mattered. The Czechoslovak history note goes into this.
          • Contested cases. Whether Czechoslovakia in 1946 belongs in the same story at all is genuinely disputed: the party there won a free election with 38 percent and had a real popular base, so an account resting only on ministry-grabbing is incomplete. The tool includes it anyway, because the pattern of which ministries the party held is the part that looks the same everywhere, and because a case that fits less well is the more useful test of the argument.
          • Cabinet composition. The list of posts is a composite of the ministries these cabinets typically contained, standardized across both scenarios so the comparison works. It is not a transcription of either cabinet. Real ones differed in their exact departments, split and merged them over time, and had ministers without portfolio and further deputy premierships beyond the one shown here. The list is ordered by conventional precedence, which is a real convention and not a ranking by importance — noticing the gap between the two is most of the point of the exercise.
          • The information ministry. Both countries had one, and in both it is priced cheaply here, which is a modeling choice rather than a fact: these were not departments other parties fought over. In Hungary the ministry was held by the Smallholders throughout, so a student who takes it outright is doing something the Communists did not manage. What they did instead was place their own political state secretary inside it, which the tool represents as a deputy placement.
          • What the model has no term for. The budget stands for the weight a party brought into the room, but once the cabinet is settled the tool scores only what those posts give you. Force applied afterward, from outside the cabinet, is not in the model at all. This matters most in Hungary. Build the cabinet the Communists actually took — interior, the deputy premiership, transport, social welfare, and their own man one level below the information minister — and it does not reach the standard the tool sets, because two of the four kinds of leverage stay thin: the party held no part of the courts, and held the press only through somebody else's ministry. That is not an error in the arithmetic. It is what the cabinet list gave them, and the rest came from somewhere the model does not look.
          Coalition bargaining in political science

          Political science models of coalition bargaining usually treat ministries as valuable because of the policy they control. On that view a party's ideal portfolio is the set of ministries closest to the issues it cares about, and the prestigious departments — foreign affairs, finance, defense — are prizes worth paying for. Most of the empirical literature on who gets what in coalition cabinets is built on some version of this assumption.

          This case points at a different conception of what a portfolio is worth. Some ministries are valuable not because they set policy but because they hand over a piece of the state: staff, files, licenses, appointments, the power to permit and to prohibit. A party that holds those can change the rules of the game it is playing rather than just the outcome of one round. That distinction — between portfolios that deliver policy and portfolios that deliver capacity, patronage, or agenda control — is now a standard move in the coalition literature, and you will meet it again when the course reaches government formation and portfolio allocation.

          The other lesson is about inference. If you knew only the published cabinet lists from 1945, and you scored them the conventional way, you would have concluded that the Communist parties came out of these negotiations badly. They did not have the top jobs. Reading those lists correctly requires a theory of what the jobs were for.
