Delegation in government

A prime minister hands each policy area to a cabinet minister, and ministers rely on civil servants to work out the details. Each time, the person handing over the task risks getting a policy she didn’t want. The guided walkthrough works through the week 13 lecture’s delegation model and its examples. Explore lets you move the status quo and both ideal points yourself. Practice gives you new delegation problems to solve, generated without end.

010

The acceptable range can run past the ends of the 0–10 scale: a principal at 9 with the status quo at 3 would accept anything from 3 to 15. The policy line draws the part past the scale dashed. The agent never proposes a policy past his own ideal point, so the final policy always lands on the scale.

    The rules of the game, in full

    Policy is a single line, and the principal and the agent each have an ideal point on it. Each prefers policies closer to their ideal point, and cares only about distance: a policy two units to the left is exactly as good as one two units to the right.

    The agent presents a policy proposal, and the principal says yes or no. Yes, and the proposal becomes the policy; no, and the status quo stays. The principal says yes to any proposal at least as close to her ideal point as the status quo, including one exactly as close. Without that last part, the agent could always propose something a little closer to his own ideal point, and there would be no best proposal to make.

    The agent knows all this, so he proposes the policy in the principal’s acceptable range closest to his own ideal point. When his ideal point is on the same side of the status quo as the principal’s, or the status quo is between them, that rule covers everything. When his ideal point is on the far side of the status quo from hers, the point in the range closest to him is the status quo itself, so policy doesn’t change. The lecture’s examples don’t include that case, and practice leaves it out, but explore will show it.

    Where these numbers come from

    The examples in the walkthrough are the ones in the week 13 lecture slides on principal-agent models. The problems in practice mode are generated by the tool. They are invented numbers, not real governments, and the tool is built so that none of them can be a lecture example or a homework question.
